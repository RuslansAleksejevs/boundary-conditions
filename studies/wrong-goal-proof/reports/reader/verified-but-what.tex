{\large A correct certificate is not the same as a correct answer\par}
\vspace{7pt}

\begin{abstract}
A proof can be correct even when it proves the wrong statement. We test whether
a model reviewer mistakes a valid certificate for an altered task as evidence
about the original. Our small, fully checkable environment uses four Boolean
inputs: every answer can be tested against all 16 possible states. We separate
ordinary model translation from deliberately planted changes, then show the same
candidate and certificate with a PASS heading, a neutral heading or an explicit
candidate-only scope warning. The original task and an instruction to check the
match remain visible in every condition. After a development pilot, the frozen
Qwen3.5-122B-A10B evaluation covered 100 new original contracts. Each presentation
correctly rejected all 40 planted substitutions and accepted all 20 equivalent
rewrites. Both paired false-SAFE differences were zero; conservative intervals
still span about minus to plus 43 percentage points. In the natural pipeline,
37 valid translations preserved the contract, while three malformed translations
and one malformed review reduced useful completion to 36/40. Two recorded
examples make the distinction concrete: a reversed implication was rejected,
and a redundant condition was accepted. The contribution is an auditable
measurement setup and a bounded negative result, not a new theory of mathematical
equivalence, a demonstrated benefit from scope labels or a general safety guarantee.
\end{abstract}

\section{Which question did the proof answer?}
Imagine a team that turns a request into a formal statement, checks that statement,
and reports back. Its checker may work perfectly while the statement quietly
changes. A reviewer must therefore ask two questions: \emph{is this evidence valid?}
and \emph{does it answer the original request?}

We isolate that handoff. Our contribution is a finite testbed with checkable
certificates, a paired comparison of three presentations of identical evidence,
and a complete record of failures as well as useful answers. The tested model did
\emph{not} make the proposed false-assurance error. That result matters as a clear
boundary condition: the original was short, unambiguous and always available.

\section{Closest related work}
SemantEq~\cite{semanteq} shows why proving two statements true does not establish
that one faithfully translates the other. Mei and Douglas~\cite{mechanical} add
numerical and vacuity checks to semantic review of formalizations. The gap between
a valid proof and a faithful statement is therefore established prior work.
Our narrower question concerns a downstream reviewer's use of fixed local evidence.
We do not reproduce those benchmarks or offer a general equivalence criterion.

\section{How the test works}
Each task fixes four true/false inputs, $p,q,r,s$, and two rules. $C$ says which
states are allowed; $Q$ says what must hold in those states. The question is
\[
  \text{Does every allowed state satisfy the requirement?}\qquad
  \forall x:\ C(x)\Rightarrow Q(x).
\]
A \texttt{SAFE} answer means yes. A correct \texttt{UNSAFE} answer supplies an
allowed state that violates $Q$. \texttt{UNRESOLVED} is permitted, but is not a
completed task. These labels describe the finite claim, not real-world safety.
We count a false assurance when the reviewer says SAFE and an exact counterexample
refutes the original.

A strict Boolean syntax supports NOT, AND, OR, implication and constants. Local
code checks all 16 states. A SAFE certificate lists each state once, its values
of $C'$ and $Q'$, and a hash of the candidate. A second evaluator uses bit vectors
to check the scalar solver's work; missing rows, duplicates, wrong hashes and
false witnesses fail. No generated code is executed.

\subsection*{Natural changes versus inserted changes}
The main set contains 40 natural tasks, balanced 20 SAFE and 20 UNSAFE. The model
is asked to translate faithfully; nothing is inserted. We compare $C$ with $C'$
and $Q$ with $Q'$ on every state, separately from the truth of the final claim.
Different wording can preserve both rules; a format error establishes neither
preservation nor drift.

The other 60 originals have fixed candidates. Forty UNSAFE originals receive
planted changes with valid SAFE certificates: restrict the allowed states, drop
a requirement, replace AND by OR, or reverse an implication (ten each). These are
\emph{injected test faults}, not observed translator mistakes or successful attacks.
Twenty SAFE originals receive equivalent rewrites. Every candidate's allowed
domain is nonempty; empty-domain proofs are tested separately in software.

Each fixed case is reviewed three times: \textbf{B}, a PASS heading; \textbf{N}, a
neutral heading; \textbf{S}, explicit candidate-only scope. The evidence is identical.
All three receive the original and the instruction to check correspondence. S has
no hidden reference answer. Heading lengths are approximately character-matched,
not token-matched; scope wording changes meaning as well as presentation.

\section{What happened}
The pilot passed the fixed interface and clean-task gates: 50/52 valid roles and
11/12 clean originals correct, against thresholds of 47 and 10. Natural completion
was 7/8; each presentation solved 8/8 planted and 4/4 equivalent cases. Its one
malformed translation was preserved. No repeat pilot or outcome-driven prompt
change was used. The main set was frozen before its first response.

Both roles used Qwen3.5-122B-A10B through OpenRouter's fixed Alibaba route:
temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, reasoning enabled, requested output limit 8,192 tokens,
and one sample per condition. This hosted alias does not pin immutable weights.
Main originals and candidates were checked for development overlap in their
Boolean functions and original formula structures; mutation families remain shared.

\section{Two actual handoffs, read closely}
These examples were selected \emph{after} the run for clarity: the shortest combined
original/candidate formulas in each of the planted and equivalent groups. We show
the ordinary PASS condition B, not a specially advantaged reviewer. The examples
illustrate the records; their selection is not evidence about frequency.

\subsection*{1. Reversing an arrow changes the task}
\texttt{holdout-fault-019}, condition B. Here $\neg$ means NOT, $\lor$ means OR,
and $\Rightarrow$ means ``if \ldots\ then.'' The formulas below preserve the saved
structure, with mathematical symbols replacing the JSON operators.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}\toprule
 & Allowed states & Required property\\\midrule
Original & $C=\neg r$ & $Q=(\neg r)\Rightarrow(s\lor p)$\\
Candidate & $C'=\neg r$ & $Q'=(s\lor p)\Rightarrow(\neg r)$\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\tablecaption{The planted change reverses the implication in $Q$; it does not
change the allowed-state rule $C$. Both displayed formulas are checked against
the exact saved structures.}
When $r$ is false, the original still requires $p$ or $s$. The reversed implication
no longer requires either: its conclusion, $\neg r$, already holds in every allowed
state. The candidate's certificate really is valid. It covers all 16 states;
eight are allowed and none violates $Q'$. The original has two violating states.

The model's actual answer, quoted exactly, is:
{\small
\begin{verbatim}
{"verdict":"UNSAFE","witness":{"p":false,"q":false,"r":false,"s":false}}
\end{verbatim}
}
At this state, $C=1$ and $Q=0$, while $C'=1$ and $Q'=1$. A valid candidate proof
and a valid original counterexample coexist. The reviewer answered the original
correctly despite the PASS heading. N and S returned the same witness. This is
\emph{not} an example of deceiving Qwen.

\subsection*{2. Saying the same thing twice is harmless}
\texttt{holdout-equivalent-019}, condition B. The allowed-state rule changes from
$C=q$ to $C'=q\land q$. The requirement is unchanged:
\[
                       Q=Q'=q\lor\bigl((p\land r)\lor(s\lor q)\bigr).
\]
Repeating the same allowed-state condition changes no allowed states. Both contracts allow
eight states and have no counterexample. The candidate again has a valid 16-row
certificate. Its syntax and hash differ from the original, but $C=C'$ and $Q=Q'$
on all 16 states. The actual B answer is:
{\small
\begin{verbatim}
{"verdict":"SAFE","witness":null}
\end{verbatim}
}
N and S also answered SAFE. Rejecting every changed formula would lose this useful
answer; a literal syntax match would reject all 20 equivalent controls.

\section{What the null does---and does not---tell us}
Under these explicit conditions, the reviewer distinguished a checked candidate
from a correct answer to the original. Scope wording showed no observed advantage.
The evidence does not show that wording never matters. Forty shared-template
faults, one model route and one sample per condition leave wide uncertainty.

The baseline was not blind. It saw the original task and was told that a proof
of a different contract does not establish that task. A harder, less explicit
interface might behave differently, but hiding the original after seeing this
null would answer a different question. There is also no no-certificate arm or
budget-matched single-agent comparison: we cannot estimate the effect of having
a certificate at all, or whether a team is better than a single agent.

Two distinctions matter for future evaluations. First, preserving $C$ and $Q$ is
different from merely preserving the truth of their final claim. Two unrelated
claims can both be true. Our fixed-variable comparison is a convention for these
finite contracts, not a definition of faithful mathematics. Second, an invalid
format is different from a false assurance. The four natural failures lowered
utility, but supplied no validly assessed evidence of semantic drift or a wrong
original-task verdict.

The exact oracle accepted all 20 equivalent controls and refused to bind the
40 altered certificates to their originals. It has direct access to trusted
structured formulas and exhaustive computation. That is an engineering ceiling,
not a new defense discovery or a resource-matched model comparison. The original
task's meaning was not hidden from the model.

\section{Conclusion}
This study produced a checkable negative result. Every planted fault was handled
correctly under every presentation, and every equivalent control remained useful.
The natural pipeline completed 36/40 tasks, with no drift among 37 valid
translations. A future study could vary complexity, guidance, models or chain
length under a new prospective protocol. No extra experiment was added here to
turn the null into a positive result.

\section{Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations}
\textbf{Scope.} Four Boolean inputs, fixed meanings, shared generation templates
and single samples are deliberately narrow. Seeds reproduce tasks and order,
not stochastic model outputs or undisclosed weight revisions. The two evaluators
share syntax validation and hashing, so a bug could still affect both.
The five thousand paired resamples use seed 606; independent-case assumptions
are not established by a synthetic generator. No presentation equivalence,
general safety, hidden reasoning mechanism or benefit from labels is demonstrated.

\textbf{Resources and preserved failures.} Development used 51 requests and
USD0.39923650 in known reported cost. Main used 259 attempts: 257 HTTP200 and
two HTTP429, with known cost USD1.99717180 for 257 attempts. Both permitted,
identical technical retries succeeded; format failures were not repaired.
The study's known total is USD2.39640830 across 308/310 attempts. Two unknown
charges retain full holds of USD1.22683392. The resulting USD3.62324222 wallet
commitment is below the USD20 allocation, but is not a final bill. Unknown is
not zero. Two main responses reported output beyond the requested 8,192-token
limit; both ended normally and fit the prospectively reserved context bound.
Detailed timing and accounting remain in the historical main report.

\textbf{Data and dual use.} All tasks are synthetic. No real service is attacked
and no external tool action is taken. Statement substitutions could inform
misleading proof workflows; publishing exact checks and useful-equivalence
controls supports auditing without claiming production protection. Only synthetic
prompts were sent. The selected provider's terms, checked 8 November 2026,
permit logging for 30 days and report no training use, not zero retention.
Hidden reasoning text, credentials and private coordination records are excluded.
The USD20 study ceiling was contributor-funded; no sponsor, grant or automatic
top-up is implied. Research-assistant session costs are separate and unmeasured.
